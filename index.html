Write-up

Communicating what the records establish

This visualization uses ProPublica’s July 2020 release of civilian complaint records obtained from New York City’s Civilian Complaint Review Board. The unit of analysis is a row in the published allegation data, rather than a unique complaint, officer, or police encounter. This distinction matters because several allegation records can belong to the same complaint. The visualization therefore identifies its counting unit explicitly and provides separate counts of distinct complaint and officer identifiers.

Records are grouped by allegation category and investigation outcome. Substantiated outcomes with different disciplinary recommendations are combined under “Substantiated.” Unsubstantiated and exonerated outcomes remain separate because they represent different findings. Unrecognized or missing outcomes are retained in an additional category instead of silently removed. The percentage view divides each outcome count by all included records in its allegation category. The count view uses the same records and a shared, zero-based scale.

Horizontal stacked bars support comparisons across categories. Consistent colors, direct percentage labels, sample sizes, definitions, and tooltips help readers interpret the marks. Percentage bars make composition visible, while the count toggle reveals differences in recorded volume that equally long percentage bars would otherwise conceal. The accompanying table provides exact counts and rounded percentages.

Transparency also requires limits. This release selects officers who had at least one substantiated allegation and excludes certain records. Complaint reporting, investigation, and publication processes shape what appears in the data. The charts describe this published collection; they do not measure the probability that an officer commits misconduct, establish whether every allegation is true, or estimate department-wide misconduct rates.

Write-Up

Turning uncertainty into reassurance

The black hat visualization uses the same allegation records and category filter as the white hat view. Its main manipulation is semantic aggregation: it combines unsubstantiated and exonerated outcomes into a single category called “Cleared.” This grouping makes a lack of sufficient evidence appear equivalent to a finding that conduct complied with department rules. Although the combined count is arithmetically correct, the invented category encourages a conclusion that the original outcome definitions do not support.

A second manipulation changes the apparent unit of analysis. The prominent “Officers cleared” label describes a percentage calculated from allegation records, not a percentage of unique officers. Officers can appear repeatedly, so an allegation-level share cannot be interpreted as an officer-level clearance rate. The headline reinforces this interpretation by presenting investigation outcomes as evidence of clearance rather than distinguishing uncertainty from exoneration.

Visual emphasis supports the misleading narrative. The combined category receives a bright, reassuring color and a large summary statistic, while substantiated outcomes are muted. A restrained dashboard layout, familiar percentage axis, sample sizes, and source attribution make the display appear credible. A small methodological footnote technically discloses the grouping but competes poorly with the headline and dominant statistic. The selected historical population also receives less prominence inside the dashboard than its broad framing would warrant.

These choices demonstrate deception through data transformation, titles and labels, and visual emphasis without changing individual source values. The reveal interaction restores separate outcome categories and corrects the denominator language while preserving the underlying records. This comparison shows why accurate arithmetic alone does not guarantee an honest visualization: readers also need appropriate categories, counting units, and contextual limits.

Project documentation

References, development, and AI use

References

  • ProPublica: Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers — the July 2020 dataset and accompanying field documentation.
  • D3.js — the JavaScript library used to construct the interactive charts.

Development process and team contributions

The application uses HTML, CSS, and a single JavaScript file. Shared data preparation functions group the allegation records by category and investigation outcome. Both visualizations use the same selected records, allowing readers to compare how different grouping and labeling choices change their interpretation.

Development focused on interpreting the outcome definitions, implementing the contrasting visualizations, and connecting the category filter, measurement toggle, tooltips, and framing reveal. The white hat view preserves the original outcome distinctions, while the black hat view deliberately combines outcomes and changes their apparent meaning.

AI usage and reflection

ChatGPT helped explore the white hat and black hat narratives, and generate the initial HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. It also suggested the framing reveal as a way to demonstrate how aggregation and labels influence interpretation.

Extra credit features

1. Reveal the framing: The “Reveal the framing” interaction transforms the black hat visualization into a more transparent presentation using the same records. It separates the misleading “Cleared” category into unsubstantiated and exonerated outcomes, corrects the allegation-versus-officer label, and replaces the reassuring headline with an explanation of investigation uncertainty. The combined percentage remains unchanged, demonstrating that the same arithmetic can support different impressions depending on its presentation. This feature extends the two required visualizations by allowing readers to inspect the manipulation directly rather than only reading about it.

2. Coordinated Category Filtering Across Both Visualizations: A shared category selector updates both charts, summary statistics, and the results table together. Readers can investigate whether the misleading narrative holds across different allegation categories while comparing the same subset of records. This extends the required comparison into an interactive exploration.

3. Percentage and Count Comparison: The white hat visualization switches between percentages and absolute counts using the same records. Percentage views reveal outcome composition, while counts expose differences in sample size that equally long percentage bars conceal. This supports the project’s ethical focus by demonstrating how the measurement chosen affects interpretation.

4. Inspectable Aggregate Results Table: An expandable table displays exact outcome counts, percentages, and category totals for the current selection. It updates with the category filter and includes outcomes too small to label inside the chart. This adds an inspection layer beyond chart tooltips, allowing readers to examine the numerical evidence behind both presentations.
